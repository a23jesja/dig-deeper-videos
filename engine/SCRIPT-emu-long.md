# The Army That Lost a War to Birds (long video, 3 min 5 s, 1920 x 1080)

## Facts (checked 2026-11-12 against en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emu_War; each line is in the article)
- After WWI "large numbers of discharged veterans ... were given land" in Western Australia to farm wheat.
- With the Great Depression (1929) farmers were encouraged to grow more wheat, "the government promising - and failing to deliver - assistance in the form of subsidies"; "wheat prices continued to fall".
- "approximately 20,000 emus"; emus "regularly migrate after their breeding season, heading to the coast from the inland regions".
- They "consumed and spoiled the crops and left large gaps in fences where rabbits could enter".
- "A deputation of ex-soldiers" met the Minister of Defence, Sir George Pearce, and asked for machine guns; he agreed, also "on the grounds that the birds would make good target practice"; "a cinematographer from Fox Movietone was enlisted".
- Major G. P. W. Meredith, Sgt S. McMurray, Gunner J. O'Halloran, two Lewis guns, 10,000 rounds.
- 2 November 1932: "some 50 emus were sighted", out of range; the settlers tried to herd them into an ambush, "the birds split into small groups and ran".
- 4 November: ambush near a dam, "more than 1,000 emus"; "the gun jammed after only 12 birds were killed, and the remainder scattered".
- Army observers: "each pack seems to have its own leader now - a big black-plumed bird which stands fully six feet high and keeps watch ... and warns them of our approach".
- Ornithologist Dominic Serventy: "The Emu command had evidently ordered guerrilla tactics".
- Truck: "unable to gain on the birds, and the ride was so rough that the gunner was unable to fire any shots".
- "By 8 November, six days after the first engagement, 2,500 rounds of ammunition had been fired"; kills uncertain, "one account estimates that it was 50 birds".
- Meredith's report: his men "had suffered no casualties". Withdrawn 8 November.
- Meredith: "They can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks."
- Parliament, November 1932: the Prime Minister "was mockingly asked ... whether a medal would be struck for the soldiers".
- Back in the field 13 November (five days after 8 November); "by 2 December, the soldiers were killing approximately 100 emus per week".
- Recalled 10 December; his report: "986 confirmed kills with 9,860 rounds, at a rate of exactly 10 rounds per confirmed kill" (disputed: "by his own count" in the script).
- Farmers "once again requested military assistance in 1934, 1943, and 1948, but were turned down".
- Exclusion barrier fencing; the bounty system: "57,034 bounties were claimed over a six-month period in 1934".
