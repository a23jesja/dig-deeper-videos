## Description
A village sued its weevils. Were animals really put on trial? Yes. Weevils were eating a village's vines, so the village took them to court. And the court gave the weevils a lawyer. He argued that God made the animals before man, and gave them every green herb to eat. So the village offered the weevils a field of their own, away from the vines. The villagers could still walk through it, and use its water. But the weevils' lawyer said no. The land was too barren for his clients. So the court sent experts to inspect the field. The verdict? Lost. The last page of the record is missing. Nobody knows. But yes: animals really were put on trial.

The case was heard in Saint-Julien in Savoy (today in France) in 1587, before the bishop's court of Saint-Jean-de-Maurienne. The insects' representatives were Antoine Filliol and Pierre Rembaud. An earlier suit, in 1545, ended with prayers instead of a verdict. The last page of the record is missing, and there is no evidence that insects ate it.

The scenes are dramatised. "They get a lawyer?", "Picky eaters." and "So who won?" are our own lines, not quotes. The villagers are not the real people of the case.

Sources:
- E. P. Evans, The Criminal Prosecution and Capital Punishment of Animals (London: Heinemann, 1906), the chapter "Bugs and Beasts before the Law" and appendix A (the records of 1587, in Latin). Project Gutenberg no. 43286.
